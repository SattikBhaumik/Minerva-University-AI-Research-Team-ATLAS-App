# ATLAS findability and trust: choice overload, onboarding, provenance, comparable tools

Research date 2026-11-23. Several hosts (nngroup.com, credibility.stanford.edu, berkeleytime.com, hydrant.mit.edu) were blocked from direct fetch in this session, so most findings come from search-result summaries, not full-page reads. Evidence strength is flagged per item. Not legal advice.

## 1. Choice overload, progressive disclosure, role filtering

### Takeaway
Choice overload is real only under specific conditions, not a universal law: the 2010 meta-analysis found an average effect near zero, and the 2015 meta-analysis found it appears when the task is hard, the set is complex, preferences are unformed, and the goal is to minimize effort. Applicants (unformed preferences, high stakes, unfamiliar product) sit on the risky side of those moderators, so the owner's cap of three options per choice and progressive disclosure are defensible as a precaution, but "max three" itself is not an evidence-derived number.

### Cited Findings
- Iyengar and Lepper's jam field study (Draeger's, Menlo Park): 3% of tasters at the 24-flavor stand used a discount voucher vs 30% at the 6-flavor stand — [Timothy Harford summary](https://timharford.com/2009/11/given-the-choice-how-much-choice-would-you-like/); original paper hosted at [UW](https://faculty.washington.edu/jdb/345/345%20Articles/Iyengar%20&%20Lepper%20(2000).pdf) (not read in full by me).
- The jam result has been hard to replicate; the lead author reportedly failed to replicate it in a 2008 dissertation attempt, and the second author failed to replicate the chocolate experiment — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/pop-psych/201602/is-choice-overload-real-thing) (secondary source).
- Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010, J. Consumer Research): 63 conditions, 50 experiments, N = 5,036; mean effect size virtually zero with considerable between-study variance — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:v:37:y:2010:i:3:p:409-425); full text [PDF](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf).
- The same meta-analysis: people with preexisting preferences or expertise were less affected; published studies reported the effect more often than unpublished ones (publication-bias signal); the authors could not identify sufficient conditions that reliably produce overload — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/pop-psych/201602/is-choice-overload-real-thing) (secondary). A "D = 0.02" figure appears in that blog only; I could not confirm it in the paper.
- Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (2015, J. Consumer Psychology 25(2):333-358): 99 observations, N = 7,202; four moderators: choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, decision goal. Higher difficulty, complexity, uncertainty and an effort-minimizing goal each increase overload. Overall effect is significant once moderators are modeled, contrary to the 2010 conclusion — [Kellogg listing](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual); search summary via [Bohrium](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/choice-overload-a-conceptual-review-and-meta-analysis/814702993403281408-7309) (secondary).
- McShane and Böckenholt (2018, Psychometrika) reanalyzed the same data: at low moderator levels the effect can reverse (more choice is better) or vanish, depending on outcome measure — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2018/multivariate-meta-analysis-with-application-to-choice-overload/?p=1) and [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/what-predicts-consumer-choice-overload).
- A 2024 review groups moderators into choice-environment factors and decision-maker characteristics — [PMC11111947](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11111947/).
- Progressive disclosure (Nielsen, 2006 definition via IxDF): "defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, making applications easier to learn and less error-prone"; main risk is that hidden features become hard to find — [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/encyclopedia/progressive_disclosure.html), [UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/what-is-progressive-disclosure/) (secondary; NN/g page itself not read).

### Inferences
- Applicants plausibly match all four risk moderators (hard, unfamiliar decision; preference uncertainty; high stakes). Current students with formed goals and a concrete task (tracker, workload) plausibly match the low-risk side. This supports role-based defaults, but I found no study testing 16-19-year-olds specifically (see Gaps).
- Practical reading of "max three": it is a conservative design rule, not a measured threshold. Evidence supports reducing complexity and uncertainty (labels that state the decision each tool serves, a recommended first tool) more than any specific count. Use "three" as a cap on options within one choice, not on total tools.
- Role filtering: prefer a default ordering or "recommended for you" emphasis with everything still reachable, rather than hiding tools. Hidden features are the documented failure mode of progressive disclosure, and an applicant who cannot find the prerequisite map may assume it does not exist. A one-time "I am an applicant / I am a student" choice is itself a two-option choice, which fits the owner rule.
- Grouping 13 tools under three headings (Explore / Plan / Decide) already reduces visible choices to 3 at the first level; each group holds roughly 4-5 tools, which is above three. Collapsed groups with one visible lead tool each would honor the rule at each level.

### Gaps
- No age-specific (16-19) choice overload evidence found; the meta-analyses I saw do not report age as a moderator in the summaries I could access.
- Original papers not read in full; Scheibehenne effect-size figures beyond the abstract are unverified.
- No direct evidence on tool-count thresholds for sidebars (the jam literature is about product assortments, not feature menus); applying it to feature navigation is an extrapolation.
- NN/g's own progressive disclosure article was not retrievable.

## 2. Onboarding, "start here" path, and navigation for 10+ items on mobile

### Takeaway
NN/g's evidence leans against first-run tutorials and toward contextual help and well-designed empty states; for 13 tools the bigger risk is hidden navigation, which NN/g found measurably less discoverable. A single visible "start here" entry plus labeled, visible navigation is better supported than a tour.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g deck-of-cards study: tutorial readers had 91% task success vs 94% for skippers, and were slower (93.49 s vs 85.17 s); n = 35 per group, differences small — [NN/g Examples of Tutorials](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/onboarding-tutorials/) (via search summary; small study, weak-to-moderate evidence).
- NN/g: users often skip tutorials and forget one-time instructions; recommends contextual help, with the help's existence visible but detail revealed on request; onboarding is justified mainly when the app needs user information up front — [NN/g mobile tutorials](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/), [mobile app onboarding](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-app-onboarding/) (search summary).
- NN/g on empty states: they communicate system status, improve learnability, and give direct routes into key tasks; a blank screen leaves users unsure the system works — [NN/g empty state interface design](https://nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design), [video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/empty-states-in-application-design-guidelines/) (search summary only).
- NN/g hidden-navigation study, 179 participants, six sites: hidden navigation is less discoverable than visible or combination navigation, with larger desktop than mobile penalty; recommend visible/combination navigation and labeling the menu — [NN/g hamburger menus](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/), [methodology](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hidden-navigation-methodology/). A third-party summary reports about 20% lower discoverability for fully hidden navigation (secondary, unverified).
- Baymard: users repeatedly missed content reachable only through horizontal tabs; tabs are a poor primary navigation, especially on narrow viewports where some tabs fall offscreen — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/avoid-horizontal-tabs). Baymard's mobile navigation benchmark details are paywalled — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/navigation-menu).
- Baymard's first-run tour specifics: I found nothing citable.

### Inferences
- Replace any tour with: (a) one prominent "Start here" entry that opens the guided explorer, (b) empty states in the tracker, compare and reflect tools that state what the tool does and offer a first action, (c) short contextual hints at the moment of use, dismissible and non-modal.
- On mobile, with 13 items do not use a bare icon-only menu or horizontal tab strip as the only route. Reasonable pattern from the evidence: a labeled "Tools" button (visible label), a sheet listing the three groups, plus 3-4 persistent bottom-bar destinations (e.g., Start, Explore, Plan, Decide). This is my synthesis, not a tested pattern.
- A search or "jump to tool" field for 13 items is a plausible findability aid but I found no evidence quantifying its benefit at this scale.

### Gaps
- No Baymard-specific evidence on first-run tours (paywalled/absent).
- NN/g tutorial study is from a general app context and small; no evidence on education or applicant audiences.
- Could not fetch NN/g pages directly; numbers come from search summaries.

## 3. Trust and provenance for an unofficial site; naming and legal considerations

### Takeaway
Credibility research says people judge sites on prominent, easily verifiable cues: a real, contactable organization behind it, verifiable information, no errors, and professional design. ATLAS's "as of" dates and source links match the strongest cues; the weak point is likely disclosure of who is behind it and how to report errors. Disclaimers alone only partly reduce confusion, so name and presentation choices matter more than fine print.

### Cited Findings
- Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility (B.J. Fogg, Persuasive Technology Lab; three years of research, over 4,500 people): include "make it easy to verify the accuracy of the information on your site", "show that there's a real organization behind your site", make the site easy to use and useful, and avoid errors of all kinds (typos and broken links hurt more than most expect) — [Stanford guidelines](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines) (search summary; only part of the list retrieved), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Web_Credibility_Project).
- Fogg et al. study of 2,684 people judging two live sites each: visual design and look were among the most-noticed elements; the study underpins Prominence-Interpretation theory (people judge what they notice, then interpret it) — [Consumer WebWatch/Consumer Reports](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/how-do-people-evaluate-a-web-sites-credibility/), [NN/g summary](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prominence-interpretation-theory/). NN/g notes the evidence rests on subjective self-reports. A 2016 lab experiment supported the theory's prominence-by-interpretation prediction — [AIS THCI](https://aisel.aisnet.org/thci/vol8/iss2/1). Samples were mainly adults in 2002-2003; applicability to 2026 teens is untested.
- NN/g (1999 Nielsen; reiterated in trust topic page): four trust levers are design quality, up-front disclosure, comprehensive and current content, and connections to the rest of the web; users expect real contact details rather than only forms or chat, and expect About pages that are clear, authentic and transparent (who you are, what you do, why you exist) — [NN/g trustworthy design](https://nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/), [communicating trustworthiness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/communicating-trustworthiness/), [About Us](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-summaries/) (search summary; the "four factors" list was partly via a third-party video summary).
- Nominative fair use: the Ninth Circuit New Kids on the Block test is the model; commonly stated elements are that the product is not readily identifiable without the mark, only as much of the mark is used as reasonably necessary, and nothing suggests sponsorship or endorsement. The Second Circuit (ISC2 v. Security University, 2016) treats nominative use as a factor in the infringement analysis, not an affirmative defense, and circuits are split — [INTA fact sheet](https://www.inta.org/?p=912), [Wikipedia: Nominative use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominative_use), [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2016/11/2016_11_16_second_circuit_expands_split__13324), [UNC JOLT](https://journals.law.unc.edu/ncjolt/?p=4816). I did not locate USPTO guidance itself.
- INTA lists a case where a radio station's use of a university's marks did not qualify because there were many ways to serve Gonzaga fans without the marks — [INTA fact sheet](https://www.inta.org/?p=912) (snippet truncated).
- "MINERVA UNIVERSITY" is a registered word mark (Reg. No. 6555656, 2021) — [Justia](https://trademark.justia.com/883/88/minerva-88388525.html). I found no Minerva brand or third-party-use policy.
- Other universities typically restrict third parties from using logos or wordmarks as their own identifier or combining marks with other elements — e.g. [Missouri Southern](https://www.mssu.edu/university-relations-marketing/Brand-Policy-FINAL.pdf), [Minnesota](https://umarcomm.umn.edu/resources/guidelines-permitting-use-university-name-and-trademarks-external-organizations) (analogy only).
- Disclaimer effectiveness: a 2026 pair of randomized experiments (Beebe, Germano and Steckel) found a disclaimer alone reduced but did not eliminate confusion; adding a required acknowledgment or typing step reduced it to a level most courts would treat as negligible; a 2024 survey of a "parody" hang-tag disclaimer found net confusion stayed near 30% — [Engelberg corpus / WIPO listing for Jacoby](https://tind.wipo.int/record/9504), [Keyes (UNH)](https://law.unh.edu/sites/default/files/media/2024-03/keyes_publication_will-bad-spaniels.pdf) (search summary; the 2026 paper's venue not confirmed).
- Facts versus expression: I did not retrieve a primary source on copyright in factual data; the CourseTable episode (below) shows a university objecting on "copyrighted data" grounds regardless.

### Inferences
- Name: plain descriptive references to "Minerva University" for identification are the strongest nominative case; avoid logos, the university's colors/typography as a lookalike, a domain or product name that reads as official, and any wording like "official", "portal", "by Minerva". Name the product ATLAS with a descriptor such as "a pathway explorer for Minerva University applicants and students (unofficial)". Strength: reasoned from the nominative elements; needs counsel confirmation and ideally a courtesy email to Minerva.
- Disclosure pattern supported by the disclaimer research: place a short statement where people see it (header or footer on every page plus first-run, not only an About page), say who built it (names or team, contact), state data source and "as of" date, and consider a one-click acknowledgement the first time for applicants, since active acknowledgement outperformed passive text in the 2026 experiments.
- Error flow: a visible "Report an error" link on each fact (prefilled with page and source) answers the Stanford "real organization" and "avoid errors" cues; a public changelog and "last verified" date answer NN/g's "current content". These are inferences from the cues; no study tested these specific widgets.
- Since ATLAS has no AI-written copy, saying so plainly is itself a provenance statement; wording must be accurate and checkable.

### Gaps
- Full Stanford list (10 guidelines) and NN/g articles not read directly (hosts blocked).
- No USPTO page retrieved; legal points are from secondary legal commentary, and the legal test differs by circuit. Jurisdiction of the team and of global users is unknown.
- No research found on trust cues for teens 16-19 or on global/cross-cultural differences.
- No Minerva trademark-use policy found.
- Evidence on correction-link and changelog effects on trust: none found.

## 4. Comparable unofficial student-built tools

### Takeaway
The comparables share a pattern: student or student-organization maintained, open source, and in several cases institutionally tolerated or backed; the cautionary case is CourseTable's 2014 block by Yale, which was followed by renaming and removal of Yale/Bluebook mentions. I could not verify exact disclaimer wording for any of them.

### Cited Findings
- Berkeleytime: course discovery and planning tool combining UC Berkeley academic information; created by Yuxin Zhu and Noah Gilmore, maintained by the ASUC Office of the CTO (student government) — [GitHub mirror listing](https://github-users.yhajoa.workers.dev/asuc-octo/berkeleytime) (secondary). The About-page affiliation wording was not retrieved.
- Hydrant (MIT): student-developed planner maintained by SIPB, MIT's volunteer student computing group; MIT Physical Education and Wellness pages point students to it; GPLv3; the wiki says projects using the Hydrant name without authorization must not be considered affiliated with SIPB, Hydrant or MIT — [GitHub sipb/hydrant](https://github.com/sipb/hydrant), [wiki](https://github.com/sipb/hydrant/wiki), [MIT PE&W](https://physicaleducationandwellness.mit.edu/my-gir). Evidence of institutional recognition (MIT pages linking to it) is from search results, not verified in context.
- CourseTable (Yale): began 2012 as a student project; Yale blocked the site in 2014, citing copyrighted Yale data, access by non-NetID viewers, and averaged evaluations; access was restored after protest and Yale conceded error in handling; the founders removed Yale and Bluebook mentions and renamed it; since 2019-2020 run by the Yale Computer Society as unpaid volunteers; open source; over 7 million requests per month — [Yale Daily News 2019](https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2019/09/18/coursetable-taken-over-by-computer-society), [YDN 2024](https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2024/01/31/coursetable-grows-in-popularity-adds-new-features), [Campus Technology](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2014/01/22/yale-admits-error-in-handling-student-site-takedown-stands-by-decision.aspx), [Higher Ed Dive](https://www.highereddive.com/news/yale-shuts-down-student-run-course-planning-site/216509/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=941684). CourseTable keeps a public changelog on Canny — [coursetable.canny.io/changelog](https://coursetable.canny.io/changelog) (listed in results, not opened).
- Staleness pattern: in 2016 U of T's Arts & Science faculty said third-party timetable tools "may not provide the latest information, and do not include important information about enrolment controls" — [The Varsity](https://thevarsity.ca/2016/07/07/the-faculty-of-arts-science-introduces-timetable-maker/). A UTM course map says it is for information only and not tied to ACORN — [UTM](https://iccit-course-map.utm.utoronto.ca/?p=10481).
- Carta: no information found.

### Inferences
- Endorsement tends to follow data quality and open governance (student-government or computing-society sponsorship, open source, public changelog), not a disclaimer. A realistic path for ATLAS: a courtesy contact with Minerva admissions or communications offering to correct errors and asking for a link or a "no objection", rather than seeking formal sponsorship that would undercut "unofficial".
- The 2014 Yale episode suggests the risk factors: using non-public data, aggregating data the institution considers sensitive, and presenting outputs as authoritative. ATLAS's use of only public data with source links lowers those risks; derived judgments (e.g., workload estimates) deserve a "calculated by ATLAS from [source]" label.

### Gaps
- Exact affiliation wording on Berkeleytime, Hydrant, CourseTable and Carta pages not retrieved (hosts blocked or not indexed).
- No evidence found on how these tools handle "out of date" data beyond the Toronto statement and CourseTable's changelog.
- No evidence found on university endorsements of these tools beyond MIT pages linking Hydrant and ASUC maintaining Berkeleytime.
