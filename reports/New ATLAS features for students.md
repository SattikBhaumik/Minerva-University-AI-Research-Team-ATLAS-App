# Build tools that make students think

ATLAS should add features that make users produce something (a guess, a reason, a budget, a dated plan) and then check it against Minerva's own published rules and numbers. For applicants, the top three are an all-in four-year cost and aid-deadline calculator, "guess first, then reveal" outcome cards, and a reflection layer on saved plans (a "why this?" note per course, three deliberately different plans, and criteria the user ranks and rates). For Minervans, the top three are a handbook-driven four-year deadline calendar with .ics export, a credit and breadth calculator wired into the existing course tracker, and a tutorial, capstone and EL199 planner. The reflection layer and an "advisor-ready" plan export serve both groups. All of this runs from public Minerva pages (the Undergraduate Student Handbook, the academic calendar, the financial aid page, the four-stop Global Immersion page) plus browser-only storage, so none of it needs accounts, analytics or generated text. The evidence behind the ranking is uneven. Applicant needs come from Minerva's own FAQ and blog plus review-site snippets. Minervan needs are mostly inferred from handbook rules, because no 2023-2026 student accounts could be retrieved. The effort ratings are my estimates, not measurements. Two cautions shape everything: effective interventions make students engage their own reasons rather than receive information, and every number ATLAS adds has a source conflict or a yearly expiry that needs a named human owner.

## What applicants and new Minervans actually struggle with

Applicants mostly cannot assemble facts that already exist. Minerva's roughly 100-question FAQ shows the pattern: many answers start with "No" (no meal plan, no sports program, no AP credit, no language courses, no individualized majors, no full-ride aid), and the cost and aid rules are especially unforgiving ([Minerva FAQs](https://www.minerva.edu/faqs/)). Aid is need-aware, loan-first, outside US federal Title IV, requires the CSS Profile (school code 6033, no fee waivers), and cannot be requested after decisions are released, with aid deadlines one week after each admissions deadline ([Tuition & Financial Aid](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/tuition-fees/)). The estimated 2027/28 cost is **$59,600** before year-one extras (visa about $740, a laptop, insurance of $700-800 per semester) and recurring ones (3+ flights and roughly two sets of visas per year) ([Tuition & Financial Aid](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/tuition-fees/)). The wider research agrees that price opacity is the main information gap. In a 2024 industry survey, 69% of first-generation students ruled schools out on sticker price, against 64% of continuing-generation students, and the authors attribute part of the gap to unfamiliarity with net price (snippet-level source) ([RNL](https://www.ruffalonl.com/blog/enrollment/beyond-sticker-shock-helping-families-see-the-true-cost-of-college/)). Minerva is 85% international ([Majors & Concentrations](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/majors-concentrations/)), so the cost-of-living and visa worries surfaced in QS's international student survey apply directly ([QS](https://www.qs.com/insights/what-will-make-international-students-choose-the-us)). Misinformation compounds the problem: third-party pages still cite a 19-student class cap (Minerva says 23 or fewer), one site claims a different accreditor than WSCUC, and another says transfers must enter as first-years while Minerva's FAQ allows up to 32 transfer credits ([Minerva FAQs](https://www.minerva.edu/faqs/); [WSCUC](https://wscuc.org/institutions/minerva-university)). Major choice is the second need. Field of study is the most common regret among US adults who pursued postsecondary education (36%) ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/gallup/211070/regret-college-choices.aspx)), and the question "Can I create my own major?" is on Minerva's FAQ, which answers that individualized majors are not possible ([Minerva FAQs](https://www.minerva.edu/faqs/)).

Minervans face a different problem: an unforgiving rule chain scattered across a document of roughly 5,800 extracted lines. Per the 2025-26 handbook, declarations are due January 31 of Years 1 to 3 and September 30 of Year 4; tuition covers a maximum of 120 attempted credits; at least 20 credits must come from outside the primary major's college; tutorial registration closes in spring of Year 3 with no switching; and housing is committed by March 31 and paid whether or not it is used ([Handbook 2025-26](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69f4c03133fa120f04215dc8/69f4c03133fa120f0421673d_Undergraduate%20Student%20Handbook%202025-2026%20v.3%20-%20Google%20Docs.pdf)). Minerva's degree-planning tools sit behind myMinerva login and the Capstone Handbook is not public, so no login-free planner combines these rules. The honest limit is that no student-stated evidence of confusion could be retrieved for 2023-2026. Reddit, Discord and student wikis were unreachable, and the one burnout post is from 2017. The Minervan ranking below is therefore a rule-based inference that should be validated with a handful of current students before anything large is built.

## Evidence says make students produce, then confront them with data

The education research favors a design principle that happens to match ATLAS's constraints. Students are substantially misinformed about earnings by major and update when shown real data, though tastes dominate the final choice ([NY Fed SR500](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr500.html)). In a community-college experiment, beliefs about course enjoyment and grades were the main drivers of major choice, and students overestimated salaries by about 13% ([NBER w23333](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23333)). Having students write how course material connects to their own goals raised the share on a STEM-degree path 2.5 years later from 55% to 69% ([UW-Madison](https://psych.wisc.edu/news/students-who-see-science-in-the-real-world-are-more-likely-to-stick-with-stem-majors/)). Career-intervention meta-analyses find effects of roughly .30-.60, with values clarification a critical element and computer-only delivery weaker than counselor-supported delivery ([Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1461503/full)). Brief contact with a relatable alumna raised women's economics majoring by 8 points from a 9% base ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/app.20180426)).

The evidence also lists what to avoid. Stanford's Carta showed that historical grade distributions lowered subsequent GPA without changing which courses students took, while workload data did no harm ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/2018/05/10/knowing-past-course-data-affects-college-students-gpa/)). College Scorecard earnings data shifted behavior most among already-advantaged students ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/blog/brown-center-chalkboard/2016/04/29/is-college-choice-impacted-by-data-in-the-college-scorecard/)). Large low-touch nudge trials (about 800,000 students) found no effect on enrollment or aid ([NBER w26158](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26158.pdf)), so reminders and push alerts are both off-brand and unproven. Two caveats limit how far this transfers. Most effects come from in-class delivery or US populations, and nothing has been tested at Minerva, so a web tool may produce smaller effects than the cited studies. The ranking therefore treats strong evidence as a reason to prioritize, not a promise of impact.

## Applicants: cost clarity first, then reflection, then honest facts

The table ranks applicant features by evidence of need, fit with the constraints, and effort. Effort is my estimate: small is days, medium is a few weeks, large is a multi-week build with ongoing curation.

| Rank | Feature | Source and currency | Effort | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Four-year all-in cost calculator plus aid-rules checklist and paired admission/aid deadline timeline. The user types in their own numbers (family contribution, home-airport flights); nothing is stored. | Financial aid page (2027/28 estimates, revised yearly) and handbook cohort tables | Medium | Conflicting figures across Minerva sources; no official net-price calculator exists, so it cannot estimate aid |
| 2 | Guess-then-reveal outcome cards: user enters a guess, then sees the published figure with cohort, n and definition | March 2026 college brochures ATLAS already uses | Small | Definitions differ by college; Arts & Humanities data ends at 2023; needs human validation of labels |
| 3 | Reflection layer: "why this?" note per course, three-futures plan set, values-weighted comparison | No Minerva data needed; extends saved plans and plan comparison | Small to medium | Weak direct evidence for the three-futures format; prompts must be human-written |
| 4 | Sourced fact cards with "as of" stamps: what Minerva does not have, residential-and-live-seminar model, accreditation timeline with links to primary documents | FAQ, accreditation page, student-experience page (undated, medium stability) | Small | Facts conflict across pages; needs an editor to own the wording |
| 5 | Rotation timeline: four stops with named civic partners and past/current cities labeled by date | Global Immersion page and dated press releases | Medium | Seoul and Berlin appear in a 2024 release but not on the current page; no cohort-to-city calendar is published |
| 6 | Interview kit beside each student story: human-written questions to ask a real student, with a local place for answers | Existing Minerva-featured stories | Small to medium | Question set needs an editor; format evidence is weak |
| 7 | Blank comparison grid against the applicant's other options, Minerva column pre-filled from sources | FAQ and Minerva's own 2016 advice to compare class size, teaching and fit | Small | Older advice source; low risk |
| 8 | Glossary, documents checklist and external-scholarship deadline list | Financial aid page; scholarships page (about 29 third-party programs, deadlines as month/day with no year) | Small | Deadlines must be re-verified each year |

The cost calculator ranks first because cost is the most evidenced applicant need, the pieces are scattered across Minerva pages, and it can be built from published numbers with the user supplying the rest. Its main risk is conflict between Minerva's own sources. The financial aid page gives a 2027/28 total of $59,600, the handbook gives cohort-specific totals ($56,200 for first years in 2026-27, falling to $46,300 for fourth years), and an older page still online shows $56,200 for the Class of 2030 with different line items and a 5-6% annual increase ([Financial Aid](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/financial-aid); [Handbook 2026-27](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69f4c03133fa120f04215dc8/6aa2bf4b099aced44e3caac4_Undergraduate%20Student%20Handbook%202026-2027.pdf); [old tuition page](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/tuition-fees-old)). ATLAS should use the current page and handbook, show ranges as ranges, and say plainly that no official net-price tool exists. Work-study (up to about $5,000 a year) is tied to presence in the rotation city and is forfeited for weeks away, which a calculator should surface rather than assume.

Guess-then-reveal ranks second because it is the cleanest operationalization of the belief-correction evidence, and ATLAS already has the data. It also solves a problem identified in the earlier outcomes research: the headline figures mean different things by college (Business and Computational Sciences count internships; Natural Sciences and Social Sciences do not) and several published breakdowns do not sum correctly. A reveal screen that shows the user's guess, the published figure, the cohort, the sample size and the definition teaches that distinction instead of hiding it. Per the Carta and Scorecard results, ATLAS should never rank colleges by outcome, never compute a "best fit" score, and should keep the reveal next to the "what a graduate does day to day" stories so the number is not the only signal.

The reflection layer ranks third on evidence and cost. The prompts ("How does this connect to something you care about?") are fixed, human-written strings, and the student writes the answers into plans that ATLAS already saves, so it is small to medium work with no data source and no stale-data risk. Plan comparison then shows the student's own notes and ratings side by side, never a computed winner. The weakest link is the three-futures format: the Odyssey-plan evidence is a small single-site study ([European Proceedings 2019](https://europeanproceedings.com/article/10.15405/epsbs.2019.11.19)), so it should ship as an optional prompt and not a required flow.

Features 4 and 5 target misconceptions. The fact cards answer stale third-party claims by dating every statement and linking the primary page, and for accreditation they should link the WSCUC special-visit report (December 2025) and action letter (February 2026) rather than summarize them, because the research team did not read those documents and cannot say what prompted the visit ([Accreditation](https://www.minerva.edu/accreditation/)). The rotation timeline needs the most editorial care: the current Global Immersion page names San Francisco, Tokyo, Buenos Aires and "a European city", the 2024 Tokyo release lists Seoul and Berlin, and the 2026 Hyderabad release relaunches that city for undergraduates ([Global Immersion](https://www.minerva.edu/global-immersion); [Hyderabad announcement](https://www.minerva.edu/announcements/minerva-university-relaunches-hyderabad-as-global-rotation-city-for-undergraduates)). Public pages also give no per-city visa guidance, so ATLAS should not imply any.

Three applicant ideas should be rejected or deferred. A personalized aid estimate cannot be built from public data. Live peer Q&A needs a human-moderated channel and breaks the no-collection rule if hosted in ATLAS, so link Minerva's own events instead. A "recommended major for you" list is passive, weakly evidenced, and would need generated text. For the interest-based college finder, show the top areas as starting points and not as verdicts: a 2022 comparison found that four RIASEC inventories often gave the same person different high-point codes ([Illinois Experts](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/what-do-interest-inventories-measure-the-convergence-and-content-/)).

## Minervans: turn the handbook into a dated, checkable plan

Minervan features are mostly deterministic encodings of handbook rules, so their main risk is correctness and currency, not editorial judgment.

| Rank | Feature | Source and currency | Effort | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Four-year deadline timeline and calendar with .ics export (declarations, lottery windows, drop week 4, tutorial registration, housing March 31, Manifest) | Handbook (AY2025-26 and AY2026-27 both public) and academic calendar page (2026-27) | Small to medium | Yearly rewrite; hashed PDF links change; calendar and handbook disagree on some dates |
| 2 | Credit and breadth calculator integrated with the existing course tracker (120 attempted-credit cap, 20 credits outside the primary college, EL199 and transfer caps, IL120 and IL181 exceptions) | Handbook sections on credits and breadth | Medium | Nuanced rules; a wrong answer can cost tuition; needs registrar or advisor validation |
| 3 | Tutorial, capstone and EL199 planner: tutorial-substitution chooser, hours tracker (120 hours = 2 credits, 240 = 4, in-semester cap 120) | Handbook tutorials and EL199 sections | Medium | Capstone Handbook is not public; approvals are case by case |
| 4 | Rotation and housing checklist plus a private city log for location-based assignment ideas | Handbook housing rules; human-written city notes, dated | Medium to large | City set has changed; per-city content needs a curator |
| 5 | Private reflection log for EL199 write-ups and course notes, in the student's own words | None needed | Small | Habits of Mind names are not public, so no pre-filled prompts; need unvalidated |
| 6 | Weekly workload planner using the 8-10 hours per course rule | Handbook credit and workload section | Small to medium | Student demand is a hypothesis; only 2017 student voice found |

The calendar ranks first because the deadlines are hard and consequential and the data is public. Declaring or revising a major, concentration or minor is due January 31 in Years 1 to 3, and the handbook warns that a Year 3 change "may result in the inability to complete all requirements within four years" and can affect cost and aid ([Handbook 2025-26](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69f4c03133fa120f04215dc8/69f4c03133fa120f0421673d_Undergraduate%20Student%20Handbook%202025-2026%20v.3%20-%20Google%20Docs.pdf)). The 2026-27 academic calendar page gives dated term events (fall classes begin September 7, 2026; Manifest May 3-24, 2027) ([Academic Calendar](https://www.minerva.edu/academic-calendar)). Each event should be labeled with its handbook year and source link. A user-initiated .ics download is a file the student chooses to save, not a notification or a data collection. Because third-party guides already disagree with the handbook (one gave November 30 for Year 4 declarations; the handbook says September 30), ATLAS should treat only Minerva documents as sources. The 2026-27 handbook and the web calendar also differ: Manifest move-out is May 28 in the handbook and May 27 on the page, and Quinquatria break is dated in the handbook but "TBD" online. Such conflicts should be displayed, not silently resolved, and the declaration deadlines were extracted from the 2025-26 edition, so they must be re-verified against 2026-27 before shipping.

The credit and breadth calculator is the highest-value Minervan feature because ATLAS already has a course tracker and plan comparison, but it is also the riskiest. The rules interact: transfer, internship and prior-learning credits count toward 120 but not toward breadth, IL120 courses count for breadth only if anchored outside the major's college and not used for the major, and cross-listed IL181 tutorials count only if not used for a major requirement ([Handbook 2025-26](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/69f4c03133fa120f04215dc8/69f4c03133fa120f0421673d_Undergraduate%20Student%20Handbook%202025-2026%20v.3%20-%20Google%20Docs.pdf)). The typical course load yields only 116-118 of 120 credits, so students must plan the remainder through EL190, EL199, transfer credit or special topics. The feature should show its arithmetic line by line and link the handbook section, labeling output "check with your advisor and the registrar" so the student, not the tool, is the decision-maker. A knowledgeable Minervan should validate the logic before release. The course lottery cannot be modeled at all, since its algorithm is unpublished.

Tutorials and EL199 come next because they are irreversible or approval-gated. Tutorials cannot be switched or added in Year 4, and the handbook lists four substitutes (a 4-credit EL199, an extra concentration course, a pre-approved elective, or two 2-credit IL120s). EL199 requires approval before starting, a supervisor, a signed evaluation and reflection assignments, and caps at 8 credits of EL199/EL190 and 16 combined with transfer and prior learning. A chooser and hours tracker encode this directly. Capstone details live in a non-public handbook, so ATLAS should cover only what the public handbook states.

The last three are lower-confidence. The city log and housing checklist respond to the "city as a campus" vagueness one 2017 student described and to the housing March 31 deadline, but they need dated, human-written city content, and the city list is changing. The private reflection log matches the EL199 reflection requirement, yet no public list of Habits of Mind exists, so ATLAS cannot pre-fill prompts, and the catalog PDF should be checked for HC names before concluding they are unavailable. The workload planner rests on a documented rule but no evidence that students want it; workload information is the benign kind of data in the Carta study. Pushing these to a later round, after student validation, is the safer sequence.

## Features that serve both groups, and the risks that cut across all of them

Two features serve both audiences and should be built once. The first is the reflection layer: the "why this?" prompt, three alternative plans and values-weighted comparison work identically for an applicant weighing majors and a first-year deciding on a concentration. For Minervans the three plans map naturally to Minerva's own structure of major in Year 1 and concentration in Year 3, which is also why a "save a full plan by the end of term one" nudge should be skipped: the observational evidence for early program entry comes from US community colleges, and Minerva students declare late by design ([CCRC](https://ccrc.tc.columbia.edu/publications/get-with-the-program.html)). The second is the "bring this to your advisor" export, an extension of the existing print and backup feature that adds the student's notes, open questions and unresolved decisions. The meta-analytic evidence says counselor involvement matters, and ATLAS cannot host a counselor, so the best it can do is prepare the human conversation ([Sage](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/careerdevelopment/chpt/career-intervention-outcomes)). A third shared item is infrastructure: extend the weekly freshness check to content hashes of the handbook, calendar and financial aid pages. The sitemap's `lastmod` is not a reliable change signal, because most of its 966 entries show a bulk-republish date of 2026-11-11, and the handbook's CDN link changes with each republish, so the link must always be resolved from minerva.edu/resources ([sitemap](https://www.minerva.edu/sitemap.xml)).

Four risks recur. Stale data: tuition, aid figures, deadlines and the handbook are rewritten yearly, so every displayed fact needs an "as of" stamp and a named owner. Source conflicts: cost figures, class size (23 versus 19), the number of rotation cities, the 91% outcome headline and several calendar dates disagree across Minerva's own pages, and ATLAS should display the discrepancy with both links. Human validation: the credit calculator, the outcome definitions and any rotation copy need review by a Minervan or staff member before release. Copy: faculty finder ideas are possible from 143 public people pages, but research areas are free text needing an editor, and faculty emails should not be copied. Third-party statistics here are mostly snippet-level, and Minerva's own stats overlap (the 87% post-first-year internship and 91% full-time-or-graduate-school figures on the career page repeat the outcomes already shown), so check for conflicts before showing both ([Career Development](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/career-development)).

## Conclusion

ATLAS's constraints turn out to be an advantage. The interventions with the best evidence (correcting misbeliefs, writing one's own relevance, clarifying values, meeting a real person) require nothing the app cannot do: no accounts, no tracking, no generated prose. The weakest-evidenced features are exactly the ones the constraints already exclude: recommenders, chatbots, nudges and rankings. The real work is editorial and operational, not technical: choosing which Minerva source wins when two disagree, dating everything, and finding a human owner for each yearly refresh.

Two findings should change the roadmap. First, the Minervan side is the bigger gap, because the rules that cost students time and tuition are public yet scattered, and no login-free planner combines them. But it is also the evidence-thinnest, so a short validation round with current Minervans should precede the credit calculator and tutorial planner. Second, applicant cost transparency, not more major information, is the highest-leverage addition, because the research ties price opacity to who gets ruled out. A sensible first release is the cost calculator, guess-then-reveal cards, the deadline calendar and the reflection prompts, all small to medium. The rotation and city features should follow only once an editor commits to keeping them current.
