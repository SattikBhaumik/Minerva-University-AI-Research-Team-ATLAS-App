# Finish ATLAS by Hardening Trust, Access, Durability

ATLAS is feature-complete enough. The best remaining work is not new tools but five low-risk hardening jobs and a handful of owner decisions. First, make the app announce view changes and survive keyboard, screen reader and 400% zoom use (WCAG 2.2). Second, protect saved plans and journals, because Safari can delete script-written storage after 7 days of Safari use without a visit to the site, so a Home Screen nudge, a "last backed up" reminder and a versioned service worker matter more than any new storage engine. Third, state who is behind ATLAS, that it is unofficial, where every fact came from and when it was checked, with a visible way to report errors, because credibility research rewards verifiable cues more than fine print. Fourth, make the 13 tools findable through one "Start here" path and three labeled groups instead of a tour. Fifth, get feedback only through channels people choose to use, since even privacy-friendly analytics still process visitor requests and sit badly with "never collect applicant data". Translation, any analytics, and contacting Minerva are owner decisions. Everything else in the final table can simply be built. Most evidence here comes from search summaries because many primary sites were blocked during research, so effort ratings are my estimates and several claims need a primary-source check before they are quoted in the product.

## Accessibility: view changes, targets and the prerequisite map are the likely failures

WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023 and added nine criteria ([W3C What's New in WCAG 2.2](https://w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)). Four matter for a view-swapping app. **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Level AA)** fails when a sticky header or sidebar fully covers the focused element. **2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum, AA)** wants pointer targets of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels or enough spacing, which threatens dense sidebar links, table-row buttons and small icon buttons. **2.5.7 Dragging Movements (AA)** needs a single-pointer alternative to dragging, and W3C's own example accepts clicking the track or a text input for a range slider ([W3C Understanding 2.5.7](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/dragging-movements)). That bears directly on guess-then-reveal sliders. **3.3.7 Redundant Entry (A)** is mostly met if saved textareas keep their content across view swaps. Note that several blogs mislabel 2.4.11 as "Focus Appearance"; that is 2.4.13 at AAA.

The biggest single risk is not a numbered criterion but the pattern of hidden-toggled views. Screen readers do not announce single-page route changes and focus stays on the control that was clicked. The consistent guidance is to move focus to the new view's heading (given `tabindex="-1"`), update `document.title` on every change, and treat a polite live region as a supplement, with Marcy Sutton's user testing finding the heading focus best received ([BBC GEL routing](https://bbc.github.io/gel/foundations/routing); [Arizona focus management](https://accessibility.arizona.edu/web-apps/focus-management)). Back and forward navigation should go through the same function, and initial load should not steal focus. The fix is one function and one test, which is why it ranks first.

The prerequisite map needs the most design care. An SVG with `role="img"`, a `<title>` and `<desc>` tied by `aria-labelledby` is among the most robust patterns ([Smashing Magazine](https://smashingmagazine.com/2021/05/accessible-svg-patterns-comparison/)), and an image-role SVG without an accessible name fails a W3C test rule ([W3C ACT rule](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/act/rules/7d6734/2022-06-23/)). But a complex graph cannot be carried in an alt string, and `role="img"` makes inner links unreachable. The recommended answer is to generate a nested text list ("Course X requires A, B") from the same JSON that draws the diagram, so the two cannot drift, and so the information is also searchable and printable. Do not encode required versus recommended by color alone; use dash style plus a label.

Testing should be layered, because automation is partial. Deque claims axe-core finds about 57% of issues by volume, a vendor figure that it contrasts with the older 20 to 30% estimate ([Deque](https://www.deque.com/blog/automated-testing-study-identifies-57-percent-of-digital-accessibility-issues)). Axe added a `target-size` rule in version 4.5 and said few other WCAG 2.2 checks can be automated without false positives ([Deque axe-core 4.5](https://www.deque.com/blog/axe-core-4-5-first-wcag-2-2-support-and-more/)). The WebAIM Million 2026 found detectable failures on 95.9% of home pages, led by low contrast (83.9%), missing alt text (53.1%) and missing form labels (51%) ([Priority Pixels summary](https://prioritypixels.co.uk/insights/web-accessibility-is-getting-worse-according-to-the-2026-webaim-million-report/)). For ATLAS that points to a Playwright run with `@axe-core/playwright` over every view, in light and dark themes and at a 320 pixel width, after opening every `<details>` since axe skips hidden content ([Playwright docs](https://playwright.dev/docs/accessibility-testing)). Add a manual pass with keyboard only, NVDA, and VoiceOver on iOS, because the audience is mobile-heavy. Reflow at 320 CSS pixels (equal to 400% zoom on a 1280 pixel screen) allows two-dimensional scrolling for data tables and diagrams ([W3C Reflow](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow)), so the compare grid and prerequisite map can qualify, but prose and forms cannot scroll sideways. The note could not retrieve sources on reduced motion or dark-mode contrast, so I rely on well-known thresholds (4.5:1 body text, 3:1 for large text and UI components) that should be checked against W3C directly. A `prefers-reduced-motion` rule that disables smooth scroll and transitions is cheap and should ship.

## Performance and offline: ship less, cache by version, and expect Safari to forget

Core Web Vitals targets at the 75th percentile are LCP at or below 2.5 s, INP at or below 200 ms and CLS at or below 0.1 ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds)). The audience justifies mobile-first assumptions. StatCounter shows mobile at roughly 50 to 60% of worldwide traffic and about 66 to 67% in India in mid-2026 ([StatCounter India](https://gs.statcounter.com/platform-market-share/desktop-mobile/india)), median mobile round-trip time is about 147 ms against 92 ms on desktop ([Treo](https://treo.sh/rtt?sortBy=max)), and no applicant-specific device data exists. Because ATLAS has no analytics, there is no field data, so lab testing on throttled mid-range Android is the only signal. A Lighthouse CI budget on LCP, CLS and total blocking time plus a byte budget on the large JSON file is cheap insurance.

GitHub Pages limits the options. Community and support statements say it sends `Cache-Control: max-age=600` on everything and allows no custom headers ([GitHub discussion 54257](https://Www.GitHub.com/orgs/community/discussions/54257)), and its Brotli support is unconfirmed. So the lever is smaller payloads: fetch the catalog JSON only when its view opens, drop unused fields, minify, shard by view if large, reserve space for async content to protect CLS, use system fonts, and chunk long renders to keep tasks under about 50 ms. A service worker is the vanilla fix for repeat visits and weak networks. MDN says a service worker is not required for installability ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps/Guides/Making_PWAs_Installable)), so the case for one is offline use and speed, not the install prompt. On a project subpath, keep `sw.js` at the site root with a matching scope, since Pages cannot send the `Service-Worker-Allowed` header (community-sourced). My recommended shape, which is reasoning and not a sourced standard, is a cache name tied to the app version, network-first for the HTML shell, stale-while-revalidate for JSON, old caches deleted on activate, and no `skipWaiting()` auto-reload. Show a small "new version, reload" notice instead, so nobody loses a half-written journal entry. A service worker never touches localStorage, so it adds no risk to saved writing and sends nothing anywhere.

Data durability deserves more attention than speed. WebKit caps script-writable storage (localStorage, IndexedDB, service worker registrations) at 7 days of Safari use without interaction with the site ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/10218/full-third-party-cookie-blocking-and-more/)). Home Screen web apps keep a separate counter and are reported to avoid the cap, but the evidence is mixed and the status in late 2026 is unverified, so it needs a real iPhone test. Switching to IndexedDB is not a fix, because the same rule covers it. What works is three cheap habits. Call `navigator.storage.persist()` after the first save and treat `false` as normal ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/persistent-storage)). Tell Safari users once, in plain words, that adding ATLAS to the Home Screen protects their notes. Keep the JSON backup as the real safety net, with a "last backed up" date, a gentle prompt after meaningful new writing, a schema version in the file, and validation on import. The File System Access save picker is Chromium-only ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_window_showsavefilepicker)), so `<a download>` stays the baseline. Wrap every storage read and write in try/catch because private modes throw. Moving between devices is the same backup file carried by the person, which keeps the no-accounts rule intact.

## Findability and trust: one front door, three groups, and a visible author

The owner's cap of three options per choice is defensible as a precaution, though it is not an evidence-derived number. The 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments (N = 5,036) found a mean choice overload effect near zero ([Scheibehenne et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:v:37:y:2010:i:3:p:409-425)), while the 2015 meta-analysis found overload appears when the task is hard, the set is complex, preferences are unformed and the goal is to minimize effort ([Chernev et al.](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)). Applicants match those risk conditions; current students with a concrete task mostly do not. The famous jam study has also been hard to replicate ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/pop-psych/201602/is-choice-overload-real-thing)). No study of 16 to 19 year olds or of feature menus was found, so treat "three" as a cap within each choice, not on total tools. Group the 13 tools under three headings (Explore, Plan, Decide) and show one lead tool per group with the rest one tap away. A one-time "applicant or current student" pick is itself a two-option choice and can reorder emphasis without hiding anything. Hidden features are the documented failure of progressive disclosure ([IxDF](https://ixdf.org/encyclopedia/progressive_disclosure.html)), and Nielsen Norman Group's study of 179 people found hidden navigation less discoverable than visible navigation ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)). So avoid an icon-only menu or a horizontal tab strip as the only route; Baymard found users repeatedly missed content behind horizontal tabs ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/avoid-horizontal-tabs)). A visible "Tools" button with a text label, plus a "Start here" entry, fits the evidence. That bottom-bar layout is my synthesis, not a tested pattern.

Skip a first-run tour. In an NN/g study of 35 people per group, tutorial readers did no better (91% task success against 94% for skippers) and were slower ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/onboarding-tutorials/)). The better-supported moves are empty states that say what a tool does and offer one first action, and short dismissible hints at the moment of use ([NN/g empty states](https://nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design)). A "jump to tool" search over 13 items is plausible but unquantified.

Trust comes from verifiable cues. The Stanford web credibility guidelines, based on research with over 4,500 people, stress making accuracy easy to verify, showing a real organization behind the site, and avoiding errors including typos and broken links ([Stanford](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines)). NN/g adds that users expect real contact details and a clear About page ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)). ATLAS already has "as of" dates and source links. The gaps are a named maker and contact, a "Report an error" link on facts, a public changelog, and the unofficial label on every page, not just About. Disclaimers help only partly: a 2026 pair of randomized experiments found a disclaimer alone reduced but did not eliminate confusion, while adding an acknowledgment step cut it much further (search summary, venue unconfirmed) ([WIPO listing](https://tind.wipo.int/record/9504)). So a one-time acknowledgment for first visits, with a persistent short label in the header or footer, is better than fine print. Say plainly that no ATLAS copy is AI-written, since that is itself a checkable provenance claim. Label derived numbers, such as workload estimates, as "calculated by ATLAS from [source]".

On the name, "MINERVA UNIVERSITY" is a registered word mark ([Justia](https://trademark.justia.com/883/88/minerva-88388525.html)). Plain descriptive use to identify the school is the strongest nominative fair use position, which generally requires that the school cannot be identified without the mark, that no more of the mark is used than needed, and that nothing suggests sponsorship ([INTA](https://www.inta.org/?p=912)). Circuits differ on how nominative use is analyzed ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2016/11/2016_11_16_second_circuit_expands_split__13324)). Practically: no logo, no lookalike colors or type, no wording like "official" or "portal", and a product name that is not Minerva's. No Minerva brand policy for third parties was found. This is not legal advice, and the maker's jurisdiction is unknown. The cautionary comparable is CourseTable, which Yale blocked in 2014 over data it called copyrighted before restoring access, after which the founders renamed it ([Campus Technology](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2014/01/22/yale-admits-error-in-handling-student-site-takedown-stands-by-decision.aspx)). ATLAS's use of only public pages with source links lowers that risk. Endorsed comparables such as Hydrant (MIT, run by a volunteer student group) and Berkeleytime (maintained by student government) followed data quality, open governance and durable maintainers, not disclaimers ([Hydrant](https://github.com/sipb/hydrant); [Berkeleytime](https://github.com/asuc-octo/berkeleytime)). So name a maintainer handoff plan.

## Feedback without analytics, language, and launch

The strict reading of "never collect applicant data" rules out analytics, even friendly ones. Plausible derives a daily visitor identifier from request data with a rotating salt ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/data-policy)), and GoatCounter itself says only that a consent notice is "probably" unnecessary and that case law is thin ([GoatCounter](https://www.goatcounter.com/gdpr)). Both process visitor requests on a vendor server. Because many applicants are 16 or 17, and the UK Children's Code treats under-18s as children, avoiding collection also avoids consent and age questions; the age of digital consent varies from 13 to 16 across the UK and EU ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/children-and-the-uk-gdpr/what-are-the-rules-about-an-iss-and-consent)). Note GitHub Pages itself may log visitor IP addresses for security ([GitHub privacy statement](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/privacy-policies/github-general-privacy-statement)), which the maker cannot turn off, so a one-paragraph privacy note should say so and say that saved plans never leave the browser. Avoid third-party fonts and scripts so no other party receives a request. Consistent feedback channels are a prefilled mailto (private), a GitHub issue template (public, tied to an account, so warn people not to post personal details), small moderated usability sessions, and asking ambassadors or counselors what applicants keep asking. Nielsen's guidance is that five users find about 85% of usability problems, in several small rounds of three to four per distinct audience ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/)). I found no credible primary evidence on how other privacy-first education tools measure impact, so the honest outcome is qualitative signals, not counts.

For a global applicant pool, the audience already reads English, because Minerva requires English proficiency, teaches in English and says Challenges must be done in English with no translation ([Minerva admissions](https://www.minerva.edu/undergraduate/admissions/); [Minerva Challenges advice](https://www.minerva.edu/blog/application-challenges-advice)). So a full translation is not needed to be usable, and plain English is the right baseline ([plainlanguage.gov](https://plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/audience)). Cheap internationalization that carries no owner risk: set `lang`, mark course codes, program names and the ATLAS name `translate="no"` (W3C found all tested translation services honored it, while attribute values are handled inconsistently) ([W3C](https://w3.org/International/questions/qa-translate-flag)), keep key text in real text nodes, format dates and numbers with `Intl`, show time zones explicitly on deadlines, and use CSS logical properties so right-to-left could be added later. A reader's own browser translation is the reader's tool, not ATLAS content, so it does not breach the no-AI-content rule. Shipping pre-generated machine translations would. The share of international students is unresolved: Minerva's About page says 100+ countries ([Minerva](https://www.minerva.edu/about)), one listing shows 23.4% international for 2024-25 and another 87.4% ([CollegeData](https://waf.collegedata.com/college-search/minerva-university/students)), and the earlier ATLAS research cited 85% from a Minerva page. Do not print a percentage until it is confirmed from the Common Data Set. No data on applicants' top countries or languages exists, so let feedback requests drive any translation decision.

Launch should be sparse and disclosed. Basic search visibility is documented and cheap: unique title and meta description per page, canonical, social preview tags, `sitemap.xml`, `robots.txt`, and a Search Console submission ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link)). Because views are `hidden` toggles in one page, check that each important view has an indexable, linkable address, which is an inference to verify. Outreach should say "we built this, invite corrections", respect each community's rules, avoid DMs to applicants and avoid tracking links. Where applicants look is unverified: Minerva's own site and blog are the confirmed channels, and Reddit, Discord and YouTube could not be checked, so the owner should observe them directly. Current students and counselors are better relays than cold applicant outreach, consistent with "never overwhelm applicants". Contacting Minerva should ask for accuracy review and a link, not an endorsement, since sponsorship would undercut the unofficial status. The published contact is admissions@minerva.edu ([Minerva blog](https://www.minerva.edu/blog/application-advice-presenting-your-accomplishments)).

## Prioritized final recommendations

Effort is my estimate: S is under a day, M a few days, L a week or more. Evidence strength reflects the research notes: Strong means a primary standard or multiple consistent sources, Moderate means credible secondary or single-source, Reasoned means my inference with little direct evidence. Rules are: R1 never collect applicant data, R2 no AI-written content, R3 never overwhelm (max three options), R4 deepen learning and agency.

## Conclusion

The research changes the finish line. ATLAS does not need more features; it needs its existing promises to be checkable. The three weakest points are all invisible from the inside: screen reader users are not told when a view changes, Safari users can lose a journal without warning, and a skeptical applicant cannot tell who built the site or how to correct it. Each has a small, standards-backed fix, and none requires collecting anything from the people ATLAS serves.

The biggest tension is between measurement and the owner's first rule. Without analytics there will be no field data on speed, adoption or drop-off, so launch quality depends on lab budgets, small usability rounds and ambassadors' reports. That is a real cost, and it is the right one to pay unless the owner decides otherwise on the record. Two caveats remain. Many sources were search summaries rather than primary pages, so exact WCAG wording, Pages caching behavior and the Safari Home Screen exemption should be confirmed first, the last on a real device. And the notes found no evidence about 16 to 19 year olds, global applicants' languages, or Minerva's stance on third-party tools, so those stay open questions that the first month of invited feedback can answer.
